The **Faraday constant** is a physical constant representing the amount of [[electric charge]] per mole of matter. It is defined as the ratio between the total electric charge $Q$ of a sample of matter divided by the [[amount of substance]] $n$ of the sample. Equivalently, it is the product of the [[elementary charge]] and the [[Avogadro number]]:
$$F\equiv \frac{Q}{n}=eN_{A}=9.648\,533\times 10^{4}\text{ C/mol}$$
### Measurement
The Faraday constant can be estimated in a variety of ways, generally through chemical means. One such way is to use [[purified water]] as a solvent and dissolve copper(II) sulfate $\ce{CuSO_{4}}$ in it. The sulfate breaks down into the [[ion|ions]] $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ and $\ce{SO^{2-}_{4}}$ in a very brief amount of time, somewhere in the order of $10^{-8}\text{ s}$, and after that, water [[molecule|molecules]] begin to bond with the ions. Since water molecules are [[polar molecule|polar]], some will bond to one ion and some to the other. You can keep adding sulfate until the solution is saturated.

The ions in the solution can be separated the electrochemical way. Partially submerge two copper electrodes in the solution. The electrodes are wired to an electric circuit that contains a [[voltmeter]] between the two, an [[ammeter]] in series, a switch and an adjustable [[electrical generator]]. Close the switch and turn on the generator to produce an [[electric potential]]. $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ ions will being to stick to the cathode. Meanwhile, $\ce{SO^{2-}_{4}}$ ions will begin to stick to the anode, but in the process bond with the copper that the electrode is made of and go back to a full $\ce{CuSO_{4}}$ molecule, breaking off of the anode in the process. In practice, this reaction causes $\ce{Cu}^{2+}$ ions to detach from the anode due to being taken by the $\ce{SO}^{2-}_{4}$. The two electrode reactions occur roughly at the same rate, so the cathode gains [[mass]] at roughly the same rate the anode loses it. More importantly, since ions are electrically charged, there is also an electric charge transfer on top of the mass one. This transfer is the key to the measurements. Define $I(t)$ as the [[electric current]] from one electrode to the other due to the charge migration. We can integrate it over a period of time $\Delta t$ to calculate how much charge is being moved:
$$\Delta Q=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}I(t)dt$$
These quantity will need to be estimated empirically by measuring $I$ over time through the ammeter in the circuit. Since you can't take infinite measurement every infinitesimal $dt$, in practice, integration is substituted by a sum over $n$ finite elements
$$\Delta Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n} I_{i}\delta t_{i}\quad\text{where}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta t_{i}=\Delta t$$
So you measure $I_{i}$ every $\delta t_{i}$ seconds. This provides the charge.

Now we need the amount of substance. Since the substance is known, we know the [[molar mass]] (technically, atomic mass), so we can simply calculate the mass that's moved and then convert it to moles. To do so, make sure you know the mass of the anode before you put it in the solution. Then, after the electrochemical reaction has been running for the desired amount of time, the anode can be weighed again. The mass difference $\Delta m$ is what we're looking for.[^1]  Once found, convert to moles using the molar mass of copper $M_{\text{Cu}}$: $\Delta m / M_{\text{Cu}}$.

The two quantities are related by are related by
$$\frac{\Delta m}{M_{\text{Cu}}}N_{A}2e=\Delta Q$$
since there's two [[electron|electrons]] ($2e$) per $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$. Using the summation for $\Delta Q$ gives us
$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} I_{i}\delta t_{i}=\frac{\delta m}{M_\text{Cu}}N_{A}2e$$
Then, using the definition of the Faraday constant, we get
$$\boxed{F=eN_{A}=\frac{M_\text{Cu}}{2\Delta m}\sum_{i=1}^{N} I_{i}\delta t_{i}}$$
Some tips:
- Try to keep the intervals between current measurements constant. If you do, $\delta t_{i}=\delta t\ \forall\, i$ and you don't have to keep track of each interval. Then, you can bring the $\delta t$ out of the sum and get $\delta t\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{i}$ instead, which is easier to work with.
- Aim for 4-5 second intervals. Unfortunately, this is a very time-consuming measurements since you need to let the electrochemical reaction run its course, so you'll need to automate the current measurements. Realistically, this will need to run for more than 24 hours. At 4-5 seconds intervals, you'll get around 20000 measurements.
- Currents should be *small*. High currents can heat up the electrodes and the solution due to the [[Joule effect]] and cause all sorts of problems. Keep the generator voltage low. For more reasons why you should keep it low, it's possible that high voltages can be cause tiny electrical arcs on the electrode's surface, which can be both dangerous and change how the reaction occurs.
- For the experiment to work, the solution needs to remain saturated for the entire duration. Since you'll be leaving the solution for >24 hours and the room temperature might change, and since saturation concentration depends on temperature, you might want to use a supersaturated solution to make sure that it's always saturated even after temperature changes.

[^1]: You might wonder why be so specific about using the anode. Wouldn't the cathode be fine too since it gains mass at the same rate the anode loses it? Well, yes, numerically they are the same. The difference is very practical. When the cathode amasses $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ ions, these have a tendency to not stick uniformly, but rather aggregate is tiny branch-like "hairs" on the surface. This is due to the "power of points" in [[Conductor|conductors]]. These hairs are thin and fragile, so trying to measure the cathode without breaking them off is almost impossible. For every hair that falls off, you lose a bit of mass, which contaminates the measurement of $\Delta m$. The anode, meanwhile, just loses pieces, and does so uniformly at that, so it's  really easy to pick up and place on a scale. Given the choice, you might as well weigh the anode and save yourself the trouble.
