A **hypothesis test** is a statistical test to check whether a statement is true or false based on a [[sample]] of values from one or more [[Random variable|random variables]], usually empirically measured or simulated. More correctly, it provides an measure of how confident we are that the statement is true of false. Hypothesis tests can take many forms and test for many different statements. They are strongly related to [[Estimator#Confidence intervals]].
### Introduction
Assume you have a sample of $N$ values from a random variable $X$. In order to perform a hypothesis test, we need to introduce a **test statistic**, which is a [[statistic]] $T=T(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})$ dependent on the sample. $T$ follows some [[probability distribution]] $\phi_{0}(t)$. However, $T$ holds only under specific circumstances. These circumstances are defined as a set of mathematical constraint that need to be met for the test to succeed. One outcome of the test is **accepting** the **null hypothesis** $H_{0}$, a statement that says that the effect, law or test subject does not hold. In other words, "accepting the null hypothesis" is a complicated way of saying "we have no good evidence to claim that the effect we were trying to prove exists and any apparent correlation is probably just [[random]] chance". This does *not* necessarily mean that the effect doesn't exist: it means that our evidence can't prove it does.

A clarification: the word "effect" should be taken very broadly. Hypotheses can take a lot of different shapes, so the "effect" can be literal, like a causation between taking a new kind of medicine and a higher-than-random chance of healing a disease, or figurative, like a parameter having the value that we want. It really depends on how the test is interpreted[^1]. Accepting the null hypothesis means that the effect we have in mind is, according to our evidence, nonexistent. Perhaps the medicine does nothing to cure the disease or the parameter has a different value from our original claim.

The other possible outcome of the test is **rejecting** the null hypothesis. In this case, we say that the effect does hold and we instead accept the **alternative hypothesis** $H_{1}$. This is an alternative statement that essentially claims how the effect works; perhaps the medicine does work or the parameter does have the value we expect.

How do we determine what outcome the test has? It is said the the null hypothesis is rejected if the value of $T$ calculated on the sample lies in the **critical region** (or **rejection region**) of $T$'s distribution, $\phi_{0}$. This region is an interval of the domain of $\phi_{0}$ (the [[sample space]] of $T$) and is determined by introducing a new parameter $\alpha$ called the **significance level** of the test:
$$\alpha=\int_{t_{\alpha}}^{\infty} \phi_{0}(t) \ dt $$
Here we chose the critical region to be $[t_{\alpha},+\infty[$, where $t_{\alpha}$ is some value in $T$'s sample space. The significance level is an [[integral]] of a [[Probability density function|PDF]], so it represents a [[probability]]; a probability of what?

![[Graph Critical region|center]]

Well, the interpretation is not that simple, for $\alpha$ is arbitrarily chosen. Recall that the critical region is needed to determine if $H_{0}$ is accepted or rejected. If one chooses $\alpha$ to be really small, then the critical region is also really small, but that also means that it's really unlikely that $T$'s value on the sample falls in it. One interpretation is that it's the probability of $T$ falling in the critical region when being measured on our sample. Thus, the smaller we choose $\alpha$ to be, the more *extreme* the value of $T$ needs to be to fail the test. That's why we call it "significance level": we can adjust it to determine how likely or unlikely the test is to pass, which in turn gives you more or less information. A strict test (very low $\alpha$) will give you a lot of confidence, because the only way you can reject the null hypothesis is with a *very* extreme value of $T$. The more extreme the value is, the less likely it's just chance, so the more confident you are that there's an actual effect going on. Conversely, a loose test (high $\alpha$) is more likely to reject $H_{0}$ and give you something to work with, but the less confident you are that the effect holds, because the bigger you make the critical region, the more likely you fall in it by sheer chance. Basically, choosing $\alpha$ is choosing how demanding you make the test: low $\alpha$ give you more confident results, but you may never pass the test because it's so demanding, thereby missing weaker correlations (perhaps the medicine works, but not *that* well; a strict test would say it doesn't work at all and you throw away a possibly useful medicine); high $\alpha$ are more likely to pass, but are more prone to false positives (perhaps the medicine is no better than placebo, but the test mistakes it as working because it has low standards).

So what happens when we complete the test? If the null hypothesis is accepted, we must accept that we don't have sufficient evidence to claim that the effect exists. If it is rejected, we claim that the effect does exists according to the alternative hypothesis. Both these outcomes are only as reliable as the significance level is low.

This is the general idea of a hypothesis test: collect a sample, choose what you want to prove and a test statistic to work with, then choose a significance level and see if the test statistic falls in the critical region. Based on that, determine whether which hypothesis you accept.

Now, this outlined a specific kind of test: a **fixed-significance level test** using the **critical region interpretation**. This is just one way of doing tests, however. There are different interpretations, like the common $p$-value interpretation, that can be used to reach similar conclusions. But at heart, all tests follow the same logic:
1. Collect a sample
2. Determine your null and alternative hypotheses
3. Choose a test statistic
4. Calculate the test statistic on the sample
5. Make some considerations on that result to accept or reject the null hypothesis

[^1]: Though in the end, it all comes back down to math.
