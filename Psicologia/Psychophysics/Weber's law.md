**Weber's law** is an empirical law in psychophysics that states that two stimuli are distinguishable not by their difference, but by their ratio. Formally,
$$\frac{\Delta I}{I}=K$$
where $I$ is the intensity of a stimulus, $\Delta I$ is the difference in intensity between stimuli and $K$ is **Weber's constant**. $\Delta I$ is interpreted as the minimum difference in intensity to perceive a difference between the stimuli.

For example, one may take the difference between two blank screens with 10 and 20 points respectively and the difference between identical screens, but with 110 and 120 points respectively.

![[WebersLaw.png]]

The absolute difference between the two is the same (10 points), but the relative difference is much smaller in the latter case:
$$\frac{20}{10}=2,\qquad \frac{120}{110}=1.09$$
The ratio is higher in the former, so the difference between the two former screens will be easier to perceive.

Despite the name, Weber's constant is not really constant. It was originally envisioned as such by Weber, but history showed that it is actually quite variable. It varies between different senses (e.g. people are generally more sensitive to [[Electromagnetic radiation|light]] changes than to [[sound]] changes) and even between different conditions of the same sense (e.g. it's harder to distinguish light intensity when the light is very weak).
