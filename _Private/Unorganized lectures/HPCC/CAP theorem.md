The **CAP theorem**, in cloud computing, states that a distributed system can be described as having three properties: **consistency**, **availability** and **partition tolerance**. The theorem states that absolute consistency and absolute availability are mutually exclusive: a distributed system may be consistent and partition-tolerant (CP) or available and partition-tolerant (AP) but not both (CAP).

Actual consistency and availability are not always absolute, in the sense of being respected at all times. The CAP theorem still applies even for imperfect consistency and availability however, as they observe a push-and-pull relationship on a spectrum: demanding higher consistency requires giving up on better availability.

Consistency may be **strong** or **eventual**. **Strong consistency** is when, after an update is made to the underlying data, all subsequent reads of that data will return the updated version. On a single machine, this is the default, but on a distributed machine, this requires update propagation on all nodes. Propagation is neither trivial nor cheap, so guaranteeing strong consistency often incurs a real performance cost, as all reads that occur *after* the update, but *before* the propagation ends must either fail or be delayed until the propagation ends. If they were allowed, the reads would have an apparently random result, as they would depend on which node the read occurs on and whether it has been updated or not. Strong consistency is useful for applications where data integrity is mandatory: a good example is banking, where you *really* don't want to have inconsistent states and make erroneous transactions. Strong consistency implies a CP system, as you are completely giving up on availability during updates. **Eventual consistency** is like strong consistency, but the reads are only guaranteed to be identical sometime in the future, not immediately after the update. This means that there is a (hopefully short) period of time in which reads may either return the old value or the new value. In practice, eventual consistency happens when you allow reads during update propagation, and is therefore a looser form of strong consistency. Eventual consistency is useful when data integrity is desired, but minor inconsistencies are tolerated as long as they are brief: a good example is likes on social media posts, where even if two users see a mildly different count for a few minutes, it doesn't really matter. What you gain with eventual consistency is performance: you never have to deny or delay a read, so from the perspective of the end user, the experience is smooth and the system has higher availability. This is an example of a mixed CP/AP system, where you give up *some* consistency to get *some* availability.[^1]

[^1]: It's still not a CAP system because you can't have both without giving up one of the two.
