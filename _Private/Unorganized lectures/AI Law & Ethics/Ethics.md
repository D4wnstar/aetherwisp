Ethics is *flexible*. It is a fluid social contract that changes over time as culture evolves. This has been understood for long: for instance, in Book II of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, they were described as having "no fixity". What is right today is not the same as it was 10, 100 or 1000 years ago.

> Moral argumentation is the process of giving, evaluating and discussing reasons to justify what is right or wrong, through dialogue that engages different moral perspectives.
> \[...\]
> Moral reflection is not a solitary pursuit but a public endeavour \[...\] We cannot discover the meaning of justice or the best way to live through introspection alone.
> \- M. Sandel in *What's the right thing to do?* p. 28-29

To evaluate moral judgments, we use several parameters. We use the idea of **justice**, which is a contextual determination of rules (not necessarily laws) which we use as a benchmark of "right" and "wrong". This is different from science, where we use the idea of **correctness**, which is the application of a formal procedure to determine if something is "right" or "wrong", and **accuracy**, which is the correspondence of a model to actual reality and is more probabilistic in nature. Either way, correctness and (to a lesser extent) accuracy are objective metrics, unlike justice which is inherently subjective and contextual.

There are different approaches to explaining ethics. One of the oldest is the **teleological approach**, rooted back in Aristotelian philosophy but continued in medieval Europe through, for instance, Thomas Aquinas. The term comes from Greek work "telos", meaning "end" or "goal". In other words, it is an attempt to explain ethics in light of the end goal of the things we do. A more recent approach is the **deontological appraoch**, from Greek work "deon", meaning "duty" or "obligation". It starts from the question "what do we have to do?". The father of this approach is Immanuel Kant, during the 1700s. Continuing through history we find the **utilitarian approach**, based on the idea that one can measure ethics as a function of the utility of an action. It asks itself "what realizes the greatest happiness?". Major proponents are Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill. In utilitarianism, the basic principle is that humans are governed by pleasure and pain: *hedonism*. Utility is defined as the property of producing pleasure, therefore the goal is to maximize utility (i.e., to produce the maximum pleasure). In this sense, there exist as so-called **felicific calculus** that attempts to evaluate actions by estimating its consequences in a formal set of parameters:
- Intensity: how strong is the pleasure of pain?
- Duration: how long does it last?
- Certainty: how likely is it to occur?
- ...

Thereby, the most ethical course of action can, in this interpretation, be determined in a mathematical manner: just maximize the sum of all this parameters with respect to the cost of the action:
$$\text{utility}=\sum (\text{benefits}-\text{costs})$$
Then you reduce everything to an optimization problem over utility. This **cost-benefit analysis** is used today in contemporary decision-making, especially in business, financial or industry application. This scientific approach leads to **social choice theory**, a theory that explains the aggregation of preferences in a community for collective decision-making.
