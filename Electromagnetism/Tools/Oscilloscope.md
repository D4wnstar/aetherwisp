An **oscilloscope** is a tool used to detect and visualize high-[[frequency]] phenomena. More so than a specific device, oscilloscopes are a class of devices designed with a common goal, which is to visualize high-frequency electrical phenomena. The classical oscilloscope is based off of a [[cathode ray tube]].
## Mechanism
### Cathode ray oscilloscope
A CRT oscilloscope employs a CRT to visualize a time-varying [[electric potential]]. It is effectively a direct application of the CRT, so there are no additional major components to speak of. The specifics are in the deflection [[Capacitor|capacitors]] used on the [[electron]] beam. The vertical capacitor is powered by the phenomenon whose potential $V(t)$ we are investigating. The horizontal one is intended to be powered by a looping potential set at a specific [[frequency]]. Since we choose the frequency, we can interpret the entire horizontal span of the electrons as a span of time. Thus, the two deflectors effectively combine to draw a $t\text{-}V(t)$ plot over a chosen quantity of time. This allows us to graphically see the shape of $V(t)$ and if we set the frequency of the horizontal capacitor to be the one of $V(t)$, we can exactly plot its [[period|periodic]] behavior.

To get this behavior, the horizontal potential can't be any periodic one. It needs to increase linearly and monotonically in time so that $V_\text{hori}(t)\propto t$ and loop back instantly at the end of each period to "reset the timer". This yields a so-called **sawtooth potential**. It is a discontinuous potential and therefore inherently unphysical. However, we can make good approximations of it. The quality of this approximation is critical to the image shown by the oscilloscope, as tampering with this potential is equivalent to tampering with the time axis of the plot.

The main issue with the sawtooth is the vertical line in between periods, which should in theory be instantaneous (i.e., discontinuous). In practice, it will never be exactly instantaneous. The trick to make it work is to simply turn off the electron beam when the capacitor resets between periods. This way, regardless of how fast the capacitor can be invert direction, the plot won't result smudged at the edges. The electrons can be blocked using the shutter of the electron gun, which turns on and off fast.

The new issue with the shutter is that for as long as the shutter is on, we're not plotting anything. In other words, we miss a bit of the start and end of the plot, around the period change. The solution is to use a so-called **trigger**, which is an electrical system that only allows sufficiently high voltages through. Anything below the trigger threshold gets blocked. By choosing an appropriate trigger, it is possible to skip some periods of the potential to be measured in order to realign the signal with the missing edges of the plot. Since the phenomena intended to be measured with an oscilloscope are high-frequency, dropping a few oscillations due to the trigger won't make any perceivable difference to the human eye.

There's a few more limitations with the CRT oscilloscope. If the frequency of the phenomenon is higher than the maximum one the oscilloscope can reach, the plot risks going [[Phase|out of phase]]. If the electron beam is too strong, the glow of the screen can make a halo and make it hard to determine exactly where the electrons are landing and thus the potential at any given point. Furthermore, excessively strong beams can damage the screen. Finally, if the transit time of the electron (the time between thermal emission and hitting the screen) is too long, it can also cause the plot to go out of phase. This is also in part due to the deflecting capacitor needing some time to adapt to the changing potential.

This last part can be quantified. The circuit powering the capacitor is an [[RC circuit]], which obeys
$$V(t)=I(t)R+ \frac{Q(t)}{C}=\frac{\Delta Q(t)}{\Delta t}R+ \frac{Q(t)}{C}$$
where $I$ is the [[electric current]], $R$ is the [[Electrical resistance|resistance]], $Q$ is the [[Electric charge|charge]] stored in the capacitor and $C$ is its [[capacitance]]. The time equation for a charging RC circuit is
$$Q(t)=Q_{0}e^{-t/\tau}\quad\text{where}\quad \tau=RC$$
If the frequency of $V(t)$ is very large compared to $1/\tau$, the signal remains out of phase with respect to the capacitor's charge.

To summarize: there's a lot of ways an oscilloscope can malfunction, almost all of which having to do with excessively high-frequency phenomena. As such, it's typical for the manufacturer of the device to provide a maximum working frequency above which the oscilloscope is not expected to function properly. This quantity is equal to $1/2\Delta T_\text{min}$, where $\Delta T_\text{min}$ is the minimum [[period]] of the oscillating phenomenon that the oscilloscope is capable of plotting. Anything lower than this will not be plotted without distortion by the device.
