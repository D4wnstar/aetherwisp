A **cathode ray tube** (**CRT**) is a vacuumed funnel-shaped glass tube containing a [[fluorescence|fluorescent]] screen on the large end and an [[electron gun]] on the small end. The gun shoots electrons to the screen, which cause it to illuminate and show and image based on where the electrons collided. By deflecting the electrons in specific ways, it is in principle possible to generate almost arbitrary images on the screen.

Cathode ray tube have had many applications in history, most commonly the [[cathode ray television]]. An application that is still in use today is the [[oscilloscope]], used in laboratories to measure time-varying [[Electric potential|electric potentials]]. The [[Braun tube]] is a predecessor device used to measure the [[electron]] [[Electric charge|charge]] to [[mass]] ratio; see also [[Electron charge-mass ratio with Braun tube]].
### Mechanism
The cathode ray tube is evacuated during construction to a very thin internal atmosphere. On the small end there is a small [[electron]] gun and on the large one there is a screen (flat or lightly curved) covered in a layer of [[fluorescence|fluorescent]] material. The basic idea is that the gun shoots electrons at the screen, where they excite the fluorescent material, leading it to produce light. The tricky part is having the gun shoot electrons in a way that represents the phenomenon we're trying to visualize.

The electron gun works like this: a circuit is attached to some power source with [[Electric potential|voltage]] usually in the range of 6 volts. The circuit contains a singular [[Electrical resistance|resistor]] intended to be heated up by the [[Joule effect]]. The resistor is covered with a small hood that is not attached to the circuit. When the resistor heats up, it emits [[thermal radiation]] that is illuminates the hood. The (pretty low [[energy]]) [[photon|photons]] interact with the material through the [[photoelectric effect]] and force it to release electrons in the surrounding space. This is the electron source. However, electrons are finite. If the hood was isolated, it would never recover the lost electrons which, besides making it chemically unstable, would also lead to it gaining a net positive [[electric charge]]. This net charge would attract the remaining electrons, making them progressively harder to break off and therefore making the source unusable.[^1] The fix is simple: provide a constant source of fresh electrons to replace the old ones. Thankfully, we happen to be sitting on an effectively infinite supply of electrons: we typically call it "planet Earth" (or "*ground*", so to say). Thus, the only thing that needs to be done is to attach the hood to the ground and let the Earth quite literally provide all the electrons in the world.

Now that we have an infinite source of electrons, we need to actually shoot them. The thermal emission is unreliable, weak and goes all over the place, so we need a way to contain it and aim it precisely where we want it. The electrons are thermally emitted with [[random]] velocity, and its magnitude is a [[random variable]] that follows the [[Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution]]. If we were to simply accelerate these electrons, the constant addition in velocity would simply translate the distribution. The velocity would still be random with the same distribution, just a higher [[mean]]. To better define this velocity, call $B_{e}$ the threshold energy required to force the hood to emit an electron, which is the [[binding energy]] of the electrons to the [[atom|atoms]]. From the theory of the photoelectric effect, we know that the output [[kinetic energy]] of the electrons is $K_{e}=E_{\gamma}-B_{e}$, where $E_{\gamma}$ is the energy of the photon that triggered the effect. We can construct the apparatus so that this energy is around one [[electronvolt]] (by choosing heating voltage, resistance and hood material). This energy is very much under the [[Lorentz transformation|relativistic]] threshold, so we can use the classical formula for kinetic energy to find that electrons on average move at
$$v=\sqrt{ \frac{2K_{e}}{m_{e^{-}}} }\simeq 600\text{ km/s}$$
Fast! But only about $0.2\%$ of the [[speed of light]]. We can do better.

We can take the outgoing thermal electrons and have them pass through an anode. Since the anode is negatively charged, electrons will be repelled and we can use this to our advantage to accelerate them. The [[electric field]] produced by the anode depends on the voltage $V_\text{acc}$ that we give it. Common values are in the range of $400\text{-}1000\text{ V}$. Going higher is possible, but requires special care, as at higher voltages you risk emitting X-ray radiation, which is [[ionizing radiation|ionizing]] and therefore dangerous to people.[^2] At acceleration voltages this high, the thermal kinetic energy $K_{e}$ because almost irrelevant, as the anode contribution completely overtakes it. Unsurprisingly, the energy of an electron accelerated by $400\text{-}1000\text{ V}$ is $400\text{-}1000\text{ eV}$, much bigger than the thermal $1\text{ eV}$ from before, and because of that, the energy/speed randomness becomes effectively nonexistent. Though fast, this is still out of relativity range, as $0.4\text{-}1.0\text{ keV}\ll m_{e^{-}}=510\text{ keV}$. Hence, we can still use the classical formula
$$v_\text{acc}=\sqrt{ \frac{2K_\text{acc}}{m_{e^{-}}} }=\sqrt{ \frac{2eV_\text{acc}}{m_{e^{-}}} }\simeq 18750\text{ km/s}\tag{1}$$
or about $6.2\%$ of the speed of light. Finally, since the CRT has been vacuumed, we can be sure that they won't collide with air molecule before the screen, which would cause them to [[scattering|scatter]].

Now we have a beam of fast electrons, but the thermal emission still goes everywhere, so most of it doesn't go inside the accelerator anode. We need to collimate the beam. To do so, we introduce another anode, shaped like a hollow cylinder. This allows it to guide the electrons. To reuse the gun metaphor, this is the "barrel" of the gun. Unlike in a gun, the barrel comes *before* the accelerator, as we want the electrons to be nice and collimated before shooting them. Focusing can be complicated business; the focusing barrel is usually called a **[[Wehnelt cylinder]]**.

An additional component that can be added is an electron shutter, which is a high-transparency grid located shortly after the heated cathode that can be turned on to generate a planar electric field. This field effectively blocks the electrons from going through for as long as it is on. It is placed near the cathode where the electrons have the lowest kinetic energy, so that the field (and thus voltage) necessary to stop them is as low as it can be. This is beneficial because low voltages are faster to produce, which allows the shutter to activate faster.

The Wehnelt cylinder collimates the electrons from the source and directs them into the accelerator. We now have a thin beam of fast electrons of a known speed. We need one last thing: to control where it's going. Electrons are charged, so the easiest thing to do is to use the [[Lorentz force]] to deflect the electrons. By placing a [[capacitor]] after the accelerator, we create a very simple electric field of known strength that we can use to control the deflection in one direction. If we put *two* capacitors, [[Orthogonality|orthogonal]] to each other, then we can control deflection in both axes of the screen.

The field of one parallel-plate capacitor with distance $d$ between plates is
$$E(t)=\frac{V(t)}{d}$$
where $V(t)$ is the time-varying potential that we give it. The Lorentz force then is
$$F(t)=eE(t)=\frac{eV(t)}{d}$$
The complication is that the trajectory becomes parabolic. The solution is that because the electron goes so fast, we can approximate the deflection as linear, at least to start. The capacitors deflect the electron perpendicular to the axis of the CRT so the [[Linear momentum|momentum]] variation must be
$$\Delta p_{\perp}=F(t)\Delta t=\frac{eV(t)}{d}\frac{l}{v_{\parallel}}$$
where $\Delta t$ is the time the electron spends inside the capacitor, $l$ is the length of the capacitor and $v_{\parallel}$ is $v_\text{acc}$ from $(1)$, renamed to clarify that it is parallel to the axis of the CRT. Through trigonometry, the deflection angle can be shown to be
$$\tan\theta=\frac{\Delta p_{\perp}}{p_{\parallel}} =\frac{eV(t)}{d} \frac{l}{v_{\parallel}} \frac{1}{m_{e}v_{\parallel}}=\frac{l}{2V_\text{acc}d}V(t)$$
For small angles, calling the distance between the gun and the screen $L$:
$$y\simeq L\tan \theta=\left( \frac{lL}{2V_\text{acc}d} \right)V(t)=S_{y}V(t)$$
where $S_{y}$ is called the **sensibility** of the $y$ axis.[^3] Clearly then, $y\propto \Delta V(t)$, meaning the linear changes in voltage will have corresponding linear changes in vertical deflection. Because the electrons shot this way will impact the screen and illuminate it, we can visually see the shape of the $V(t)$ as higher potentials will shoot higher (bigger deflection $\Delta y$) compared to weak ones. Negative potentials will go in the opposite direction (negative $\Delta y$). In practice, we made a graphical [[voltmeter]] for time-varying potentials. This concept is used to build [[Oscilloscope|oscilloscopes]]. A more advanced usage is for [[cathode ray television|cathode ray televisions]].

[^1]: This is called **cathode poisoning**.

[^2]: Old CRT televisions used specially made lead oxide glass that blocked ionizing radiation just so they could reach upwards of $\sim 2500\text{ V}$ without risking the viewer's health. This is also part of the reason why they were so heavy, as there could several kilograms of lead in each TV.

[^3]: Using $y$ as the coordinate. Use $x$ or $z$ for the other capacitor.
